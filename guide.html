Chapter 1

Preconception basics

Preconception care is simply the health care and habits you put in place before you conceive. It matters because the earliest weeks of pregnancy — when major organs form and the neural tube closes — often happen before a pregnancy is confirmed. Optimizing your health in advance gives that critical window the best possible start.13

A short checklist to start

  • Book a preconception visit. ACOG recommends a prepregnancy counseling visit to review your history, medications, vaccinations, and any chronic conditions such as diabetes, thyroid disease, or high blood pressure.1
  • Start folic acid now. All women who could become pregnant should take 400 mcg of folic acid daily, ideally beginning at least 1 month (often 1–3 months) before conception.14
  • Review medications and supplements with your clinician — some need adjusting before pregnancy.1
  • Update vaccinations (e.g., MMR, varicella, flu) as advised before conceiving.1
  • Aim for a healthy weight. Even a modest 5% change in body weight can restore more regular ovulation in those with irregular cycles.12
  • Stop smoking and alcohol; limit caffeine. There is no known safe level of alcohol around conception.1

The 90-day window. An egg takes roughly three months to mature before ovulation, so the lifestyle choices you make over the next ~90 days can influence egg quality and your hormonal environment. This is why "start early" is the theme of everything that follows.11

Common questions

How long does it usually take to conceive? Most people who have regular, unprotected intercourse conceive within a year. Because fertility evaluation timelines depend on age, ACOG recommends starting an evaluation at 12 months of trying if you're under 35, or at 6 months if you're 35 or older — sooner still if you already know of a fertility-affecting condition.18

Do I need to stop birth control first? Fertility typically returns quickly after stopping the pill, patch, ring or hormonal IUD. It can take a little longer to resume regular cycles after the birth-control shot. There's no need to "detox" beforehand — just start folic acid before you stop.3

My cycles are irregular — does that matter? Irregular cycles (including with PCOS) make it harder to predict ovulation but don't rule out conceiving. It's worth mentioning to your clinician at your preconception visit — see Chapter 2 for what you can influence, and use the cycle tracker on your dashboard to spot patterns.12

When should I see a fertility specialist? Sooner rather than later if you're 35+, have known PCOS, endometriosis, or thyroid disease, very irregular or absent cycles, or a partner with a known fertility factor. Otherwise, the timelines above are a reasonable guide.18

Chapter 2

Hormone optimization — what's in your control

You can't micromanage your endocrine system, but several powerful levers are genuinely within your control. Reproductive hormones don't operate in isolation — they're tightly linked to your blood sugar, thyroid, stress response, and sleep. Optimizing those foundations is the most reliable "natural" way to support balanced hormones and regular ovulation.5

Your menstrual cycle, in brief

A quick refresher on the hormones behind your cycle makes the rest of this chapter easier to use — and it's exactly what the cycle tracker on your dashboard is built around.19

  • Follicular phase (day 1 → ovulation): starts on the first day of your period. Falling estrogen and progesterone trigger FSH, which recruits egg follicles; estrogen then rises as one follicle matures and rebuilds the uterine lining.
  • Ovulation: a surge in luteinizing hormone (LH) triggers release of the egg, roughly 28–36 hours after the surge begins. This — plus the days just before it — is your fertile window.
  • Luteal phase (ovulation → next period): the follicle left behind becomes the corpus luteum and produces progesterone, which prepares the uterine lining. This phase is fairly fixed at ~12–15 days; it's mainly the follicular phase that varies in length between people.

Why cycle length varies: because the luteal phase is fairly constant, a shorter or longer cycle usually means a shorter or longer follicular phase — which is exactly why ovulation-day estimates get less reliable the more irregular your cycles are (common with PCOS). Track a few cycles to see your own pattern rather than assuming a textbook 28 days.

Insulin & blood sugar — the highest-yield lever

Stable blood sugar means steadier hormones and more regular ovulation. Strategies with the best evidence are dietary (fiber, protein and fat with each meal), movement (a short walk after eating, and regular resistance training), and adequate sleep — all of which improve insulin sensitivity.514 For women with PCOS, improving insulin sensitivity is often the single most impactful change.12

Thyroid

Thyroid hormone supports both ovulation and early fetal brain development, so it's worth confirming your thyroid is well-managed before pregnancy. In one study of women undergoing fertility treatment, a preconception TSH in the higher end of "normal" (2.5–4.5 mIU/L) was associated with differences in outcomes — a reminder to test and, if you have a thyroid condition, optimize it with your clinician before conceiving.13 Adequate iodine (see Chapter 5) is essential for thyroid function.8

Cortisol, stress & sleep

Your stress-response and reproductive systems share wiring. Chronically elevated cortisol can interfere with the hormonal cascade that triggers ovulation, and short sleep independently disrupts cycle-regulating hormones. Prioritizing 7–9 hours of sleep and a daily stress-reduction practice is a legitimate, evidence-aligned fertility strategy — not a "nice to have."5

Bottom line: you optimize hormones indirectly. Nail blood sugar, thyroid, sleep, stress and a healthy weight, and balanced reproductive hormones tend to follow. Beware supplements marketed to "balance hormones" without evidence — food, movement, sleep and stress are the levers with the strongest support.5

Chapter 3

Nutrition & diet

There's no single "fertility diet," but a nutrient-dense, whole-food pattern that keeps blood sugar stable supports both hormone balance and the nutrient stores pregnancy draws on. ACOG advises screening your diet to make sure you're meeting needs for calcium, iron, vitamin A, the B vitamins (including folate), vitamin D and other nutrients.12

An eating pattern that works

  • Build every plate around protein, fiber-rich carbohydrates and healthy fats to blunt blood-sugar spikes.5
  • Load up on folate-rich foods — leafy greens, legumes, citrus — alongside your supplement.6
  • Include iron sources (lean meats, lentils, spinach) with vitamin C to boost absorption.10
  • Eat omega-3 rich fish (salmon, sardines) 1–2× weekly for DHA, choosing low-mercury options.2
  • Don't skip choline — eggs and lean meats are top sources; it supports neural-tube closure alongside folate.9
  • Include iodine and calcium from dairy, seafood and iodized salt.8

Food first, supplement to fill gaps. Whole foods deliver nutrients in forms and combinations supplements can't fully replicate. Supplements (Chapter 5) are there to guarantee the time-sensitive essentials like folate — not to replace a good plate.2

Chapter 4

Exercise & movement

Regular, moderate exercise improves insulin sensitivity, supports a healthy weight, lifts mood and lowers stress — all of which help ovulation and hormone balance. The goal is consistency, not extremes.11

How much

  • Aerobic: about 150 minutes of moderate activity per week (e.g., 30 minutes of brisk walking, 5 days) — or 75–150 minutes of vigorous activity — in line with WHO and ACOG guidance.115
  • Strength: add two resistance-training sessions per week. These improve insulin sensitivity and, in PCOS, have been shown to lower the free-androgen index and raise sex-hormone-binding globulin.12
  • Recovery: gentle movement like yoga or mobility work supports stress and sleep.

PCOS & irregular cycles

Exercise is especially powerful here. A pilot randomized trial found exercise training improved reproductive outcomes in women with PCOS, and benefits appear to go beyond weight loss alone — likely via improved insulin sensitivity.12 Where weight loss is a goal, even a 5% reduction combined with exercise can help re-establish ovulation.12

Moderation matters. Too little activity contributes to insulin resistance, but overtraining can itself signal stress to the body and disrupt cycles. For most people, moderate and consistent beats intense and erratic.11

Chapter 5

Vitamins & supplements

A daily prenatal (or preconception) vitamin helps guarantee the time-sensitive nutrients pregnancy needs from day one. Below are the nutrients with the strongest preconception evidence, with typical targets. Always confirm doses with your clinician — especially vitamin D and iron, which are best guided by blood levels.2

Preconception vs. prenatal: a "preconception" formula and a prenatal overlap heavily; the non-negotiable is adequate folate started before conception. Some fertility-focused formulas add CoQ10, which has preliminary evidence for supporting egg health — discuss with your clinician.2

More is not better. Some nutrients (e.g., vitamin A as retinol) are harmful in excess around pregnancy. Stick to a reputable prenatal and evidence-based doses rather than stacking high-dose single supplements.17

Chapter 6

Sleep, stress & environment

Sleep & stress

Covered as hormone levers in Chapter 2 — and worth repeating because they're so often neglected. Protect 7–9 hours of sleep on a consistent schedule, and build a daily practice that lowers cortisol: a walk, yoga, breathwork, journaling, or time outdoors. Chronic, unmanaged stress is a real fertility variable, not just a mood one.5

Substances

  • Alcohol: best avoided when trying to conceive — no safe level is established around pregnancy.1
  • Smoking & vaping: stop before conception; both harm fertility and pregnancy.1
  • Caffeine: moderate intake is generally considered acceptable; many clinicians suggest keeping it modest (around ≤200 mg/day) when trying to conceive — ask yours.1

Endocrine-disrupting chemicals (EDCs)

EDCs are substances that can interfere with hormone systems. You can't avoid them entirely, but sensible, low-effort reductions are reasonable while trying to conceive:16

  • Avoid heating food in plastic; favor glass or stainless steel.
  • Choose fragrance-free personal-care and cleaning products where practical.
  • Wash produce; reduce known high-pesticide exposures where you can.
  • Reduce handling of thermal receipts (a BPA source).

Keep it proportionate. The biggest, best-evidenced wins are still folate, blood-sugar/insulin, sleep, movement and not smoking or drinking. Treat EDC reduction as sensible housekeeping, not a source of anxiety.16

Chapter 7

Emotional wellbeing & support

Trying to conceive can bring up anxiety, grief, or strain on relationships — even before any diagnosis of infertility. This is common, it's not a sign you're "doing it wrong," and it's worth taking as seriously as the physical side of preparation.20

What's normal to feel

Research on infertility-related distress describes five recurring challenges: anxiety, low mood, a hit to self-esteem or identity, strain on the relationship with a partner, and a shrinking support network as friends' pregnancies or the topic itself feels hard to be around. You don't need a diagnosis of infertility to feel any of this — the uncertainty of trying, monthly disappointment, or family and financial pressure are enough on their own.20

When to reach out for support

  • Talk to your clinician early — ACOG guidance encourages screening for mental health as a routine part of preconception and fertility care, not an afterthought once things feel dire.20
  • If you already live with anxiety or depression, it's better to get support in place before starting active trying or treatment, not after.20
  • Therapy works one-on-one, as a couple, or in a group — and several medication options for anxiety/depression are considered compatible with trying to conceive and pregnancy; this is a conversation for your prescriber, not something to decide alone.20
  • Your partner (if you have one) is going through this too — even when the physical preparation falls mostly on you. Naming that out loud early tends to help.

Use the notes field on your dashboard as a low-stakes place to track mood alongside sleep and cycle day — patterns are often easier to see in writing than in memory.
